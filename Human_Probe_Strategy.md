# Human Probe Strategy

## Standard-mode benchmark at an 8-guess budget:

On the standard-mode benchmark with 400 secrets (seed 1, the same sample the README uses for its baseline comparisons), HumanProbe with its default of 2 opening probes solved 100% of games at 5.485 average guesses blind, and 4.787 with the answer list available. That lands between the two existing baselines on average guesses -- worse than GreedyLetters (5.407 blind) and better than RandomConsistent (5.610 blind) -- while actually reaching a higher solve rate than either of them (GreedyLetters 99.5%, RandomConsistent 99.2%, HumanProbe 100%).

## Why 100% instead of close to 100%:

GreedyLetters and RandomConsistent's handful of failures aren't from random noise -- they're the single-position-family failure mode documented in the README (`_ATER`, `_AKER`, `_IGHT`, and similar). Both solvers pick every guess from the current candidate pool, so against a secret word like LATER they can easily land on another family member such as WATER; that guess returns 4G 0Y and eliminates exactly one candidate, degenerating into a slow linear search through the family that an 8-guess budget doesn't always survive. HumanProbe's two probes are chosen before any filtering happens, purely by letter coverage across the whole word list, so they don't have a way to be pulled from inside whatever family the secret happens to belong to -- a family's shared letters (A, T, E, R for the `_ATER` group) compete for coverage against the entire alphabet and rarely win. By the time probing ends, HumanProbe has already learned how many letters from two broad, unrelated groups are in the secret, information that is independent to "which family is this", rather than another guess drawn from inside the trap. This is the same mechanism the README credits for why the one-step family eliminates these failures entirely -- probing with a word that isn't pulled from the current candidate pool is what breaks the linear search -- and HumanProbe gets a partial version of that same protection for free, purely as a side effect of committing to fixed, non-adaptive probing guesses.

## Tuning num_probes:

The probe count was swept from 0 to 6 on the same 400-secret sample, and performance moves in one direction only -- the opposite of what a real-Wordle habit would predict. 0 and 1 probes tie for the best average (5.377), 2 probes costs 5.485, and every additional probe makes things steadily worse: 5.812 at 3, 6.207 at 4, 6.651 at 5, and 7.016 at 6, with the solve rate also dropping at the high end (95.5% at 6 probes versus 100% at 2). In real Wordle, committing 2-3 of 6 guesses to fixed probes works because every guess returns labelled, per-letter feedback, so a blind probe is never wasted. Word500's feedback is an anonymous count, and candidate filtering (inherited from Solver) already extracts everything available from any guess, probe or not -- so a probe's actual cost is that it ignores everything learned so far in favor of a precommitted word, while the closing phase always guesses from the current, already-narrowed candidates. Every extra probe is one more turn spent not using the information already on hand, a cost real Wordle's labelled feedback absorbs for free and Word500's anonymous feedback cannot.

## Overall Takeaway:

The default is kept at 2 probes despite 0 and 1 scoring slightly better, because the point of this solver is to demonstrate the strategy itself rather than to chase the smallest average. The result is exactly the finding worth recording: the "probe first with a couple of broad-coverage words, then deduce" habit that serves real Wordle players well does not transfer to Word500's anonymous feedback, and the more of the guess budget spent probing, the more that cost compounds. This sharpens the README's existing observation that "strategy matters less than expected" among the one-step scorers -- here, strategy (or rather, an unhelpful human habit carried over from a different game) matters quite a lot, just in the wrong direction.
